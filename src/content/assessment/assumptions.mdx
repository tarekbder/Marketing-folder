## What the poster asks for

<Card title="Poster box 6" kicker="Riskiest assumption" tone="purple">
**If this is wrong, the model fails.** In the talk: *explicitly state your riskiest assumptions. What must be true for this to work, but is currently unproven?*
</Card>

The lectures don't cover this element directly, so this page is built mostly from the guidelines, the rubric and the poster, with supporting ideas from the lectures.

## Find the existential risk

The rubric separates two kinds of risk:

<Grid cols="2">
  <Card title="Existential commercial risk" kicker="What Excellent looks for">If it's wrong, nobody buys, nobody pays, or it can't be reached profitably. *"Independent shop owners will pay a monthly fee for this."*</Card>
  <Card title="Minor operational risk" kicker="Caps you at Good" tone="pink">Annoying but fixable. *"Our supplier might be late."* *"The website could be slow."*</Card>
</Grid>

A useful test is the poster's own line: **"If this is wrong, the model fails."** If the venture could survive the assumption being wrong, it isn't the riskiest one.

Questions that often surface it:
- **Desirability:** do customers really have this pain, acutely enough to switch?
- **Viability:** will the payer pay this price, this often? (See the peer probe "Who actually pays for this?")
- **Reachability:** can you reach the persona through your channels at a cost that works?
- **Timing:** why would they buy **now** instead of later?

## Mark what you know, and what you don't

The poster asks you to mark **every** entry:

<p>
  <span class="epa e">E</span> <b>Evidence</b>: backed by data you have ·
  <span class="epa p">P</span> <b>Plausible</b>: reasoned, not proven ·
  <span class="epa a">A</span> <b>Assumption</b>: a belief you haven't tested
</p>

Your riskiest assumption will usually be one of the entries marked **A**. Being honest here is what the rubric rewards as "deep self-awareness".

## Supporting ideas from the lectures

- **The biggest startup risk is not learning fast enough** ([lectures 1](/topics/01-marketing-fundamentals/#1-orientation) and [2](/topics/02-positioning/#1-setting-the-stage)). Naming the assumption is the first step to testing it quickly.
- **Personas start as assumptions** and get refined through research and interviews ([lecture 5](/topics/05-customer-journey/#icp-and-personas)). Your first 100 customers differ from your 10,000th.
- **Data → insight needs checking.** Use AI or a partner to poke holes and look for contradicting data ([lecture 2](/topics/02-positioning/#2-rapid-insight-extraction)), and remember correlation isn't causation ([lecture 6](/topics/06-roi-decision-making/#1-performance-language)).
- **Channel and budget assumptions** need testing too: a paid test budget of about 2–3× the ticket price, and no universal if-then rules for which channel works ([lecture 4](/topics/04-channel-strategy/#1-channel-selection-and-economics)). Budgets rest on assumptions about growth and conversion rates that get corrected once real data arrives.
- **Behaviour doesn't automatically follow the product.** Journeys break where trust, habits or handovers don't line up ([lecture 5](/topics/05-customer-journey/#3-journey-design)).

## How it's graded

| Excellent (1.0–1.3) | Good (1.7–2.3) |
|---|---|
| Explicitly identifies the **single most dangerous** assumption. Shows deep self-awareness of what is currently unknown or risky. | Identifies assumptions, but focuses on minor operational risks rather than existential commercial risks. |

<Callout tone="warn" title="Common mistakes">
- **Presenting the proposition as flawless** → Insufficient.
- Acknowledging risk only superficially, or **assuming customer behaviour will automatically align with the product** → Sufficient.
- Listing many small operational risks instead of picking the single most dangerous one → Good at best.
- Naming a risk without saying what must be true, or how you'd find out.
</Callout>
