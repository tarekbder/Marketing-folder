## What the poster asks for

<Card title="Poster box 2" kicker="From: brand strategy canvas" tone="purple">
**While … offer …, [target] choose [us] as … so they can …** In the talk: *state your one-sentence positioning statement clearly.*
</Card>

This box owns the **sentence**. The job behind it (needs, gains, the pyramid) lives on [Value propositions](/assessment/value-proposition/).

## What the course says

### The template
The poster uses the exact value proposition template from [lecture 2](/topics/02-positioning/#value-proposition). It's one sentence covering audience, problem and solution, written for internal alignment, not as a feature list.

<ValueProp />

- **Competitive space:** who the alternatives are, what they offer, and for whom.
- **Core promise:** your target audience, their problem, your brand as their category/niche, and the key benefit.

### Two worked examples

> **SumUp:** While traditional commercial banks and legacy acquirers offer complex, contract-locked card terminals for large retailers and hospitality venues, independent shop owners and sole traders who aren't ready to commit to long-term contracts or expensive hardware choose SumUp as their flexible all-in-one payment solution, so they can accept card payments instantly with zero long-term commitments and focus on the craft they love, worry-free.

> **Fitbit Air:** While smartwatch and fitness tracker brands offer high-end sport wearables for fitness and longevity optimizers, everyday health-conscious people who are exhausted by digital noise and high-maintenance devices choose Fitbit Air as their affordable screenless wellness band that quietly monitors their well-being in the background, giving them peace of mind 24/7 so they can focus on what really matters.

Notice the contrast in both: the alternatives are complex / high-end / for someone else; the customer's problem is named; the benefit is an outcome, not a spec.

### Check it before you present
From [lecture 2's red-teaming checklist](/topics/02-positioning/#5-stress-testing-red-teaming):
- Does anything sound too generic or unspecific?
- Do the customer problem and the solution **contrast** each other?
- Could a competitor's name be swapped in? (The same test the lecture applies to the brand territory.)

You can also paste your draft into an AI chat and ask it to find gaps and misalignment.

### Keep it consistent
The sentence should match the rest of the poster: the target from box 1, the differentiation from box 4 (your sliders' white space usually shows up in the core promise) and the job in box 5.

## How it's graded

| Excellent (1.0–1.3) | Good (1.7–2.3) |
|---|---|
| The positioning statement is **sharp and commercially viable**. | Accurate, but lacks strong tension or urgency. |

<Callout tone="warn" title="Common mistakes">
- **Describing the product instead of the value or job to be done.** The rubric calls this out explicitly: it scores Sufficient at best.
- No clear statement at all → Insufficient.
- No tension: the competitive space and the core promise don't contrast.
- A list of features squeezed into a sentence.
- A generic target ("people who want to be healthier").
</Callout>
