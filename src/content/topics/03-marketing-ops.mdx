### Before the stack: a target audience check
The session opened with red-teaming each other's brand strategy canvases. Two lessons carried over:
- Real brand strategy work uses far more research than the classroom version: typically 60–80+ distinct data points, not 4–5.
- A target audience must be specific enough that both the team and potential customers immediately recognise the fit. "A product for adults" says nothing; "for adults who lecture at a university" is instantly relatable. Use a **primary audience** plus **sub-audiences** for tailoring sales, ads and content. How big the group must be depends on ambition: venture-scale startups need larger addressable markets than side projects.

### Considerations when building a stack
- Match the stack to the **business context and stage**
- **Naming conventions** for assets, campaigns, reports and UTMs
- The **minimum required process**: don't over-engineer
- **Fit-for-purpose** use of AI
- Plan for both the **short term** (weeks to months) and the **long run** (a year)
- **Decentralise** when possible to allow parallel work; centralise where compliance or governance requires it

### Risks of a badly managed stack
- Resource drain (money, time)
- Attention and focus (maintenance, clean-up, reporting)
- Website loading times and UX: tracking tools slow pages down and cost conversions
- Data noise and misalignment: **no single source of truth**
- Slow execution (confusion, governance, inefficiency)
- Bad decision-making

### The six layers
Read the stack from backend to customer-facing:

| # | Layer | What it does | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Data management** | Captures and organises raw customer data | Data warehouse, purchase logs, CRM, CDP |
| 2 | **Tracking & analytics** | Collects and visualises performance data | Google Analytics, BI dashboards, GTM, pixels |
| 3 | **Productivity & orchestration** | Keeps workflows running efficiently | Asana, Jira, Make, Zapier |
| 4 | **Production & enablement** | Creates content and documentation | CMS (WordPress, Shopify backend), copywriting tools, sales materials |
| 5 | **Experience & transaction** | Where customers interact | Shop, landing pages, lead forms, A/B testing |
| 6 | **Distribution & acquisition** | Delivers content and attracts customers | Ad platforms, social, search, email |

Three tools worth knowing:
- **Google Tag Manager (GTM):** a container code on the website that lets marketers add tracking pixels without a developer for every new tool.
- **UTM parameters:** URL query strings (`utm_source`, `utm_campaign`…) that connect clicks and conversions back to a marketing source.
- **Product feeds:** structured files listing every product's metadata (price, images, attributes) that power dynamic and carousel ads on Google, Meta and others.

**APIs** let platforms talk to each other: when a shop sends you to PayPal at checkout, the two communicate via an API.

### The attribution problem
Ad platforms like Meta and Google Ads both claim credit for the same conversion because they use different attribution windows and models. One company added up all Google Ads revenue and all Meta Ads revenue and got **more revenue than the company was actually making**. There's no perfect fix: run experiments, build a custom attribution model, or consolidate data in a neutral tool (like Google Analytics) as your own source of truth. Lecture 6 goes deeper.

<Callout>
AI is your very capable intern. The critical point for a human is **before anything customer-facing goes out**.
</Callout>

- **Transactional emails** (cart abandonment, confirmations): a human writes the template once, automation sends it.
- **Marketing content** (newsletters, social posts): a human reviews AI drafts before publishing.
- **Campaign optimisation:** a human approves new campaign variations before launch.

### AI use cases in marketing
Three reasons to use AI: **reduce effort**, **increase value** (better quality) and **unlock new capabilities** (for example launching a fully branded e-commerce shop in a day with AI-generated visuals).

<Callout tone="warn" title="Watch out">
Don't overdo it or over-engineer it too soon.
</Callout>

**In practice:** an automated email campaign ran three AI agents in sequence: a *product expert* picked relevant products for the campaign topic, a *copywriter* drafted the email, and a *compliance manager* checked health claims. A human triggered it with a Slack message naming the topic and approved the final draft before sending.

<details>
<summary>Suggested answers (based on the lecture's examples)</summary>

1. **Automation.** A clear rule-based trigger. A human writes the reminder template once; automation sends it.
2. **AI + automation.** AI drafts the content within a defined process; a human reviews every draft before it's published.
3. **AI + automation.** The steps are known (trigger → enrich → score); AI adds the enrichment. The lecture uses this exact case as its "mostly deterministic" example.
4. **Agentic.** The path varies with performance, competition and season. A human approves new campaign variations before launch.

## 3. Evaluating a stack

### By maturity

| | Validation | Early scale | Growth | Enterprise |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Revenue** | Under €500k | €1M–€5M | €10M–€50M | High volume / public |
| **Tools** | ~5–10 | ~10–25 | ~20–40 | 50+ |
| **Typical set-up** | Lightweight LP, basic CRM, GTM + GA4, website/shop, 1–2 socials | Full CMS + apps, full-feature CRM, automations, robust reporting | CDP, robust DWH architecture, BI, advanced attribution, multi-language, ERP integrations | Complex enterprise suites, governance, robust attribution models, localised instances |

<Callout>
This is **not** a prescription. Use it as a general reference. D2C e-commerce usually needs more tools than B2B lead gen.
</Callout>

Early-stage founders often don't need a full CRM: a Google spreadsheet can track the first 10–50 customers until automation and sequencing become necessary.

### Questions to ask about every tool
- **Is it a duplicate?** (two A/B testing tools, several BI platforms)
- **Can it be automated?**
- **Is it actually used?** Remember: two-thirds of paid capabilities go unused.
- **Which tool breaking would freeze the business?** Usually the CRM for lead-based companies and the CMS/shop platform for e-commerce.

## 4. A few words on GDPR

GDPR (in German: DSGVO) applies to early-stage ventures too. Fines come in two tiers:

| Tier | Maximum fine |
|---|---|
| Lower | Up to €10 million or 2% of total annual revenue |
| Higher | Up to €20 million or 4% of total annual revenue |
| Non-financial | Processing bans, data deletion, etc. |

From day one:
- **Active consent**: checkboxes, double opt-in (DOI), cookie consent, unsubscribe
- **Right to be forgotten**: a deletion process, automation and logs
- **Avoid manual access** to customer data: no downloading or sharing files of personal data

## Key terms
- **Martech stack**: the set of tools a company uses for marketing, organised in six layers
- **CRM**: customer relationship management system
- **CDP**: customer data platform
- **DWH**: data warehouse
- **GTM (Google Tag Manager)**: a container on the site that lets marketers add tracking pixels without a developer
- **UTM parameters**: URL tags that attribute traffic to a source, medium and campaign
- **Product feed**: a structured product file that powers dynamic ads
- **Single source of truth**: one agreed place where performance data is decided
- **Deterministic workflow**: a predefined rule-based process; needs automation, not AI
- **Agentic workflow**: AI decides if, when and how to act
- **Human in the loop (HITL)**: a person reviews or approves at a defined step, especially before customer-facing actions
- **Waste / core / question / target zone**: the four value-vs-effort zones for evaluating tools
- **GDPR / DSGVO**: the EU data protection regulation
- **Double opt-in (DOI)**: a sign-up confirmed twice, usually via an email link
