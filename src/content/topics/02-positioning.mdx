## 1. Setting the stage

### Which would you buy?
The lecture opens with two wearables compared row by row: materials, water resistance, battery, sensors, health features and subscription. On specs alone they look close. Then two rows are revealed:

| | Option A | Option B |
|---|---|---|
| Display | AMOLED touchscreen | No display (app only) |
| Average price | ~€1,000 | ~€500 |

Only then are the names shown: **Garmin Fenix 9 Pro** and **Oura Ring 5**. The point is that people don't choose on feature lists. They choose on what a product *means* to them, and that meaning is what brand strategy designs.

### Where strategy fails
<Callout>
A brand strategy usually fails for one of two reasons: **it's irrelevant** (it doesn't connect to what customers care about) or **it's not actionable** (nobody can turn it into decisions).
</Callout>

### Three layers
| Layer | What it defines |
|---|---|
| **Brand strategy** | Who the brand is and how the customer should perceive it |
| **Brand assets** | How that identity is codified and expressed to the customer |
| **Marketing strategy** | How the value is delivered into the customer's hands |

### Learn fast
The biggest risk a startup faces is **not learning fast enough**. Work quick-ish, use good data, generate learnings and adapt.

**In practice:** a full brand turnaround for a 7–8-year-old declining brand took about six months: 2–4 months of research, then about two months turning it into propositions, territories and strategy. The CEO, CMO, an advisor and a product lead each did the exercise in parallel, then compared interpretations. Research inputs included customer, purchase and website behaviour data, interviews with customers *and* non-customers, a survey of ~1,000 people, AI-assisted desk research, investor benchmarks, and internal interviews across product, marketing and finance. In hindsight, 2–3 months would have been enough to start seeing results.

## 2. Rapid insight extraction

<Canvas highlight="top" />

The top row of the canvas:
- **Target group**: who is it primarily for? Sub-groups? Who is it *not* for?
- **Key user/market findings**: customer and market research data points
- **Key insight(s)**: the data translated into an insight statement

<Callout>
Even a broad target group needs a qualifier (profession, sport, education…) and a "who it's not for" trade-off.
</Callout>

### Types of research

<ResearchGrid />

| | |
|---|---|
| **Primary** | 1st-party data you collect yourself |
| **Secondary** | 2nd/3rd-party data collected by others |
| **Quantitative** | Numerical: tells you *what* |
| **Qualitative** | Verbal: tells you *why* |

**Main methods:** customer survey, market survey, focus groups, interviews (customers and non-customers) ★, user testing, event-based/tracked data ★, Google (Search, Trends, Keyword Planner), desk research, social listening and reviews, experiments (painted door, ads, landing pages) ★, investor data (benchmarks, market scoping).

### Fast track with AI
- Ask AI to find and link relevant data points about the category
- Feed AI social-listening signals (Reddit, reviews) about your brand or competitors
- Use it to poke holes: check against competitors and look for contradicting data

This is a shortcut for exercises. For real decisions, rely on higher-quality, actual behavioural data.

### From data to insight
An insight is not a data point. It is what the data *means*. Read it both ways: data → "which means that…" → insight, and insight → "as can be seen by…" → data.

<DataInsight
  data={[
    "64% of fitness band users rank battery life as essential over high-density interactive screens.",
    "Two main causes for abandoning wearables: notification friction and daily charging."
  ]}
  insight="Wearable users want a hassle-free, distraction-free experience."
/>

<DataInsight
  data={[
    "Main reasons for taking supplements: overall health (65%), immune system (61%), deficiency (58%).",
    "Most important product property across all age groups: \"scientifically proven\" (43%)."
  ]}
  insight="Although supplements are classified as food by EU law, consumers evaluate them as medicine."
/>

<Exercise title="Rapid insight extraction">
1. **Fast-track research** (15 min): ask AI for data points; find a few reviews or Reddit threads and have AI analyse them.
2. **Turn data into insights** (10 min): group similar data points and extract 2–3 insights about your audience/market.
</Exercise>

## 3. Competition & differentiation

### Why not the 2×2 matrix
<TwoByTwo />

- **Problem 1:** we always place ourselves top-right.
- **Problem 2:** we bias our thinking early. Picking two axes up front hides the many others (a real category has ~20 usable ones).

### The fix: sliders
Use **four universal sliders** plus **two category-specific tensions** that come from your research.

<Sliders />

| Slider | One end ↔ other end | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Target audience | Specialized ↔ Universal | women ↔ adults; health optimizers ↔ casuals; freelancers ↔ businesses |
| Product scope | Core ↔ All-in-one | few metrics ↔ many; single vitamin ↔ multi; key solution ↔ service suite |
| Authority source | Institutional ↔ Relatable | medical ↔ lifestyle; certificate ↔ experience; banks ↔ neobank |
| Perceived value | Accessible ↔ Premium | self-service ↔ managed; budget-friendly ↔ luxury; pay-as-you-go ↔ commitment |
| Category tension 1 | e.g. Discreet ↔ Statement piece | |
| Category tension 2 | e.g. Distraction-free ↔ Engagement-rich | |

<Callout>
Phrase **both ends positively**, so neither side reads as "good" vs "bad".
</Callout>

Map competitors on each slider, then your own venture. The **white space**, where no competitor sits, is your opportunity.

**Example: Fitbit Air vs Garmin, Whoop, Oura.** Fitbit Air's white space was *lifestyle* (authority), *affordable* (value), *discreet* and *distraction-free* (category tensions). Differentiation statement:

> While others offer high-end sport wearables for athletes, Fitbit Air is a **distraction-free**, **everyday** fitness tracker that **effortlessly** fits in your daily routine.

**Example: a CRM for midwives.** Sliders ran from home-visit midwives ↔ everyone around birth, core feature ↔ all-in-one, community ↔ institutional, plus pricing. White space: an *on-the-road companion* rather than a back-office tool, serving *midwife and patient* rather than midwife only. All-in-one may be needed eventually, but the core feature is the entry point.

### Competitor circles
1. **Direct competitors**: same product, same customer.
2. **Similar but weaker or differently positioned** players.
3. **Attention-takers**: don't offer the same product but compete for the same attention.

Competition depends on the market. At SumUp, *cash* was the first direct competitor in Germany but not in the UK. SumUp's UK TV ads also looked like insurers' and banks' ads, so customers lumped all small-business services together, an attention-fatigue problem. If your market seems competitor-free, look at services for adjacent groups (e.g. apps for other patient groups or for parents).

### Practical notes
- **Time horizon:** position for now up to 1–2 years out, then redo the exercise. Five-year positioning is too speculative.
- **Product vs marketing:** the same tensions can shape features at idea stage, or sharpen communication for an existing product.

<Exercise title="Differentiation">
1. **Identify your sliders** (10 min): relabel the 4 universal sliders; add tags for 2 category sliders.
2. **Map competitors** (10 min): find ~3 competitors and place them on each slider.
3. **Map your venture** (10 min): place yourself, mark the white space and write your differentiation statement ("While others do X, I do this").

Peer feedback: questions only, aimed at the work, not the person.
</Exercise>

## 4. Exploiting the gap (positioning)

<Canvas highlight="bottom" />

### Needs & gains
| | |
|---|---|
| **Needs** | The core friction (tasks, problems) the customer tries to resolve. Can be functional, physical or emotional. |
| **Gains** | The positive outcomes or aspirational benefits the customer hopes for beyond solving the problem. |

Follow-up reading: **Jobs To Be Done (JTBD)** by Clayton Christensen. The classic case is McDonald's milkshakes: the job customers hired them for was a *morning commute break*, not breakfast.

**Netflix:** need = selection fatigue; gain = opening the app to something immediately watchable; product properties = the algorithm and like system.

### The positioning pyramid
<Pyramid />

Read it bottom-up: product properties deliver on needs and gains, which feed the brand topics, which add up to the brand territory.

| Floor (top → bottom) | Definition | Rule of thumb |
|---|---|---|
| **Brand territory** | The conceptual space we occupy in customers' minds (internal phrasing) | 2–3 words. Fails if a competitor's name could be swapped in. |
| **Brand topics** | Core themes/expertise we consistently communicate to match customer priorities | 3–4 repeatable pillars with enough content depth. 5–7 is too many. |
| **Customer needs & gains** | The customer's internal problem space and desired outcome | |
| **Key product properties** | Features/specs/capabilities that deliver on those needs and gains | |

**Example: Fitbit Air**

<Pyramid example="fitbit-air" />

| Floor | Content |
|---|---|
| Territory | The distraction-free health companion ("companion", not "coach", which implies nagging) |
| Topics | Mental well-being · (active) lifestyle · healthy fitness habits |
| Needs & gains | Smartwatches disturb my focus · smartwatches are too bulky to sleep with / I want my wearable to be stylish · I don't know what to do when metrics are off / I want expert advice on my data |
| Properties | Screenless, long battery, no sound · ultra-thin, interchangeable bands · Google Health app with AI coach |

### Value proposition
One sentence covering audience, problem and solution. It is for internal alignment, not a feature list.

<ValueProp />

> **Competitive space:** While [competitors/others] offer [product category/niche] for [their target audience],
> **Core promise:** [our target audience] who [customer problem] choose [brand/product] as their [product category/niche] so they can [key benefit/solution].

**SumUp:** While traditional commercial banks and legacy acquirers offer complex, contract-locked card terminals for large retailers and hospitality venues, independent shop owners and sole traders who aren't ready to commit to long-term contracts or expensive hardware choose SumUp as their flexible all-in-one payment solution, so they can accept card payments instantly with zero long-term commitments and focus on the craft they love, worry-free.

**Fitbit Air:** While smartwatch and fitness tracker brands offer high-end sport wearables for fitness and longevity optimizers, everyday health-conscious people who are exhausted by digital noise and high-maintenance devices choose Fitbit Air as their affordable screenless wellness band that quietly monitors their well-being in the background, giving them peace of mind 24/7 so they can focus on what really matters.

**AI check:** paste your needs/gains, topics or value-prop draft into an AI chat to find gaps and misalignment.

<Exercise title="Positioning">
1. **Bridge customer & product**: fill in the needs & gains floor, then the product features that answer each group.
2. **Brand topics**: write 3 topics you'd group your communication around.
3. **Brand territory**: write a ~5-word statement for the spot you claim, then your value proposition.
</Exercise>

## 5. Stress testing (red teaming)

Question a partner's brand strategy:
- Does anything sound too generic or unspecific?
- Are the insights aligned with the pyramid?
- Do the territory and topics address the core functionality and the customer needs & gains?
- Does the white space on the sliders clearly separate from the competition?
- In the value proposition, do the customer problem and the solution contrast each other?

## Key terms
- **Brand strategy**: who the brand is and how customers should perceive it
- **Insight**: what the data means; a statement derived from data points
- **Primary / secondary data**: collected by you / collected by others
- **Quantitative / qualitative**: what (numbers) / why (words)
- **Slider**: a positively-phrased spectrum used to map competitors
- **Category tension**: a category-specific slider drawn from research
- **White space**: an open position on the sliders no competitor occupies
- **Customer needs / gains**: friction to resolve / outcomes hoped for
- **JTBD**: the "job" a customer hires a product to do
- **Brand territory**: the 2–3-word conceptual space you claim
- **Brand topics**: 3–4 communication pillars
- **Value proposition**: one-sentence alignment of audience, problem and solution
- **Red teaming**: deliberately critiquing a strategy to find weaknesses
